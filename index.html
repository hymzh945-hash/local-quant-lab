Handbook

Six short chapters. Read them before you tune parameters — they cover most of the usual traps.
1. Look-ahead bias: the number one reason backtests flatter

Look-ahead bias means using information at a decision point that could not have been known at the time. The three common forms: filling at the same bar's close that generated the signal, reading an indicator value that only exists after the bar closes, and treating parameters that looked best in hindsight as if they had been chosen in advance.

The rule here: a signal is generated at the close and executed on the next bar. You pay one bar of delay for it, and in exchange you never get the illusion of having sold the exact top.

2. Why Sharpe lies

Sharpe divides average return by the standard deviation of returns, and it carries three built-in flaws. First, it assumes returns are roughly normal — fat-tailed markets make it read high. Second, it is wildly unstable on short samples: a few dozen observations can produce a fake 2.0. Third, it does not distinguish upside from downside volatility, so a violent strategy and a calm one can score identically.

That is why this tool also reports Sortino (which penalises downside only) and Calmar (CAGR ÷ max drawdown). Reading all three is far more reliable than Sharpe alone.

3. Out-of-sample and walk-forward

Out-of-sample means the data that played no part in choosing the parameters. This tool reserves the last 30% by default, but a single split is still fragile — if that 30% happens to be one-directional bull market, the conclusion distorts.

Walk-forward goes further: pick parameters on an earlier window, validate on the window immediately after — which took no part in that choice — then roll forward and repeat. Only when most folds are positive out of sample does a strategy qualify as roughly stable. One or two profitable folds is usually luck.

4. What funding really costs

Perpetual futures settle funding every 8 hours. If you are long, a positive rate means you pay and a negative rate means you get paid; shorts are the opposite. A single settlement is usually a few basis points, but three times a day and over a thousand times a year — a persistent long can lose a meaningful slice of return in a bull market.

This tool uses the exchange's published settlement history period by period. Only the earlier range that the contract's history does not reach falls back to the flat estimate you enter. The fallback range is flagged in the summary so you can judge how much to trust the conclusion.

5. Leverage and liquidation: the risk backtests skip

Leverage magnifies the payoff and compresses the survival room. At 2x, a 50% adverse move wipes you out; at 10x, only 10% is needed. Real liquidation also involves the maintenance margin rate, mark price, and the difference between isolated and cross margin.

This tool approximates isolated-margin liquidation with the maintenance margin rate: when account equity falls below notional × MMR, the position is declared liquidated and trading stops. It is a conservative approximation, not an exact simulation — but it is enough to show you the case that matters: beautiful backtest returns on leverage that would in reality have been wiped out long ago.

6. Overfitting: is this parameter set just lucky?

Overfitting means the parameters only work on this particular slice of history. Three ways to spot it: check whether the heatmap shows a broad block of similar colour (robust) or isolated bright cells (fitted); check whether a small change in a neighbouring parameter causes a cliff-edge collapse; check whether the out-of-sample period continues the in-sample edge.

Practical advice: do not pick the brightest cell in the heatmap. Pick a centre point whose entire neighbourhood looks decent. The first is fitted to history; only the second has a chance of continuing.
